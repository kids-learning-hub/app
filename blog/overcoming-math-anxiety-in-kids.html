Why Kids Say "I Hate Maths" — and How to Rebuild Their Confidence

KL Kids Learning Hub · Updated October 2026 · 12 min read

When a child says "I hate maths", it rarely means they lack ability. Much more often it means maths has started to feel unsafe: a place where they might look slow, be wrong in front of others, or feel lost. That feeling has a name, math anxiety, and the good news is that parents can do a great deal to reduce it.

1. What math anxiety looks like

Math anxiety is a stress reaction to numbers and maths situations. Researchers have found that worry takes up part of the working memory, the mental space we use to hold numbers while we calculate. So an anxious child genuinely has less room to think, makes more mistakes, and becomes more anxious. Breaking that circle is the goal of everything below.

What you may noticeWhat your child may say
Stomach aches or tiredness just before maths homework"I don't feel well, can I do it later?"
Rushing and guessing to get it over with"Is it 12? No, 15? I don't know!"
Freezing in front of a question they could answer yesterday"My brain just went blank."
Tears, anger or tearing up the paper"I'm stupid. Everyone else gets it."
Refusing to try when not sure of the answer"I'm just not a maths person."

Anxiety can appear in strong pupils too. Perfectionist children who are used to being right are sometimes the most frightened of making a mistake.

2. Why it happens

  • Time pressure and public answers. Being asked to answer quickly in front of others is the most common trigger.
  • A gap in the foundations. Maths builds like a staircase. If one step is missing, for example place value or the times tables, everything that follows feels confusing, and the child concludes that they are "bad at maths".
  • Messages from adults. Sentences like "I was never good at maths either" are meant kindly, but they teach the child that maths ability is inherited and fixed.
  • The belief that there is only one right way. When a child's own method is rejected, they stop thinking and start trying to guess what the adult wants.

3. What to say, and what to avoid saying

Small changes in language have a big effect, because they change what the child believes about mistakes and ability.

Instead of…Try…
"I was bad at maths too.""Maths can be tricky. Let's work it out together."
"Come on, it's easy!""This one needs some thinking. Take your time."
"No, that's wrong.""Show me how you got that." (Often the method is almost right.)
"You're so clever!""You kept going even when it was hard. That's how your brain gets stronger."
"Hurry up.""Getting it right matters more than getting it fast."
Teacher's noteWhen a child gives a wrong answer, asking "How did you get that?" in a calm, curious voice is the single most useful habit. It tells the child that their thinking matters, and it shows you exactly where the misunderstanding is.

4. Rebuilding confidence step by step

  1. Find the level where your child succeeds. Go back until the work feels easy, even if that is a year below their class. A 10-year-old who panics at fractions may simply need secure division facts first.
  2. Start every session with success. Begin with two or three questions you know your child can answer. Confidence first, challenge second.
  3. Keep sessions short. Ten calm minutes are worth more than forty tense ones. Stop on a good note.
  4. Use objects and pictures. Coins, beads, drawings and number lines make abstract ideas visible and reduce the load on working memory.
  5. Treat mistakes as information. Try a "favourite mistake of the day": look at one error together and work out what it teaches.
  6. Remove the timer. Accuracy and understanding first. Speed comes naturally later.
  7. Bring maths into play. Board games with dice, cooking, shopping and card games give children maths practice without the feeling of being tested.
Try this tonightAsk your child to teach you something they already know well, such as counting in fives or doubling numbers. Being the expert for five minutes is a powerful confidence boost.

5. A calmer homework routine

  • Choose the same time and quiet place each day, after a snack and a short break.
  • Read the instructions together and ask your child to explain the task in their own words.
  • Start with the easiest question to warm up.
  • Act as a coach, not a teacher: ask questions ("What do you know already? What could you try?") rather than giving the answer.
  • If tears start, stop. Write a short note to the teacher explaining where your child got stuck. That is useful information for them, not a failure.

6. Working with your child's teacher

Teachers often see a different child from the one at home. Share what you notice: which topics cause stress, whether timed tests are a trigger, what helps at home. Ask which skills are coming next so you can prepare gently in advance, and whether your child could sometimes answer in writing instead of out loud. A short, friendly conversation usually leads to small adjustments that make a big difference.

7. When to look for extra help

Most math anxiety improves with patience and the steps above. Talk to the school, and if needed a health professional, if:

  • the distress lasts for months or spreads to school in general;
  • your child has physical symptoms such as stomach aches or poor sleep before maths lessons;
  • after age 7 or 8, your child still struggles to compare small quantities quickly or to remember very simple facts despite regular practice. In a few children this can be a sign of dyscalculia, a specific learning difficulty that responds well to the right support.
ImportantThis article gives general educational advice. It does not replace an assessment by a qualified professional if you are worried about your child's wellbeing or learning.

Frequently asked questions

What is math anxiety in children?

Math anxiety is a stress reaction to numbers and maths situations. It uses up part of the working memory children need to calculate, so they make more mistakes, which increases the anxiety. It is common and it can be reduced.

How can I help my child who hates maths?

Go back to a level where your child succeeds, start each short session with easy questions, use objects and drawings, remove time pressure, and respond to mistakes with curiosity by asking how they got their answer.

Is math anxiety the same as dyscalculia?

No. Math anxiety is an emotional reaction, while dyscalculia is a specific learning difficulty with number sense. They can occur together. If difficulties with very basic number facts continue despite regular practice, talk to the school or a qualified professional.
