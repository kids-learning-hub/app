How to Boost Your Child's Focus and Attention Span — Games and Daily Habits

KL Kids Learning Hub · Updated October 2026 · 10 min read

Concentration is not a fixed trait that children either have or lack. It is a skill that grows with age, practice and the right conditions. Parents can help a great deal by setting realistic expectations, removing distractions, and playing games that quietly train attention and working memory, the mental "notepad" children use to follow instructions and solve problems.

  2. Set up a space that helps focus

1. Realistic attention spans by age

Expecting a six-year-old to concentrate for an hour leads to frustration on both sides. As a rough guide often used by teachers, children can focus on a single, moderately interesting task for a few minutes per year of age:

AgeTypical focused stretchPractical session length
4–5About 8–15 minutesShort activities, changed often
6–7About 12–20 minutes15-minute blocks with a movement break
8–10About 16–30 minutes20–25 minutes, then a short break
11–12About 25–35 minutes30 minutes, then a short break

These are averages, not targets. A child absorbed in LEGO may play for an hour, while the same child struggles after ten minutes of handwriting. Interest, tiredness and difficulty all change the picture.

2. Set up a space that helps focus

  • One workspace, always the same: a table with good light, the materials ready, and nothing else on it.
  • Screens away: phones and tablets out of sight, television off. Even a silent phone on the table attracts attention.
  • Quiet, but not silent: some children work better with calm background noise; others need silence. Watch what works for yours.
  • A visible plan: a small list of what to do today, ticked off as it is done, keeps the mind on track.

3. Eight games that train attention and memory

  1. Kim's game: put 6–10 objects on a tray, look for 30 seconds, cover them, and remember as many as possible. Then remove one and ask which is missing.
  2. Simon says: listening carefully and stopping yourself when "Simon" is not said trains attention and self-control.
  3. Memory pairs: classic card matching builds visual memory and patience.
  4. Listening walk: walk for five minutes in silence, then list every sound you heard.
  5. Repeat the rhythm: clap a rhythm, your child repeats it, then add one more clap each round.
  6. Spot the difference pictures and simple mazes: slow, careful looking.
  7. Statues: dance to music and freeze completely when it stops. Great for active children.
  8. Going shopping memory game: "I went to the market and bought an apple", "…an apple and some bread"… each person adds an item.
Try this tonightPlay three rounds of Kim's game with objects from the kitchen. Start with five objects and add one each time your child succeeds. It takes five minutes and directly trains working memory.
Teacher's noteChildren who struggle to follow multi-step instructions in class are often not inattentive by choice; their working memory gets full. Giving instructions one at a time, or writing them down, helps them immediately, and memory games strengthen the skill over time.

4. Daily habits that make concentration easier

  • Sleep: tired children cannot focus. Primary-school children generally need about 9 to 12 hours a night.
  • Movement: physical activity during the day, and a few minutes of movement before sitting down to work, improve concentration.
  • Regular meals and water: hunger and thirst are underrated distractions.
  • One thing at a time: doing homework while watching videos trains the brain to switch, not to focus.
  • Read together: following a story for ten or fifteen minutes is excellent attention practice.
  • Boredom is allowed: children who are always entertained have fewer chances to practise sustaining their own attention.
  • Calm breathing: three slow breaths before starting a task helps children settle. Practise it as a game first.
About fast-paced screensVery fast videos and games constantly reward quick switching. They are not harmful in small doses, but children used to them may find slower activities like reading harder at first. Balancing them with calm, longer activities helps.

5. How to talk about focus

  • Praise the effort to concentrate: "You kept going for fifteen minutes even though it was hard."
  • Avoid labels: "You're so distracted" can become part of how a child sees themselves. Describe the moment instead: "Your mind wandered, let's bring it back."
  • Teach a reset routine: when attention drifts, stand up, stretch, have a sip of water, and return.
  • Celebrate progress: track how long your child can focus on homework this week compared with last month.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a child be able to concentrate?

As a rough guide, a few minutes per year of age on a single task: about 8–15 minutes at 4–5, 12–20 at 6–7, 16–30 at 8–10 and 25–35 at 11–12. Interest and tiredness change this a lot.

What games improve a child’s concentration?

Kim’s game, memory pairs, Simon says, repeating rhythms, statues, spot the difference and listening walks all train attention and working memory in a playful way.

How can I help my child focus on homework?

Use the same quiet workspace, remove screens, plan short work blocks with breaks, let your child move before starting, and give instructions one at a time.
