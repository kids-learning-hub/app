Managing School Stress and Supporting Restless, Active Learners

KL Kids Learning Hub · Updated October 2026 · 11 min read

Some children worry about every test. Others cannot sit still for ten minutes of homework. Many do both. School stress and restlessness look different, but they often respond to the same things: predictable routines, short focused work, movement, enough sleep and calm adults. This guide explains what parents can do at home, and when it is worth asking for professional advice.

1. Signs of school stress

BodyBehaviourWords
Stomach aches or headaches on school mornings; trouble falling asleep; tirednessIrritability, tears over small things, avoiding homework, reluctance to go to school"I'm bad at everything", "The teacher hates me", "I'll fail"

A little stress before a test is normal and can even help concentration. It becomes a problem when it lasts for weeks, spoils sleep or appetite, or stops a child from enjoying things they used to like.

2. What causes it

  • Gaps in understanding. When a child does not understand a lesson, every following lesson feels harder, and worry grows.
  • Overloaded schedules. School, homework, tutoring and activities can leave no time to rest or play.
  • Pressure about results. Comparisons with siblings or classmates, or a focus on grades rather than effort.
  • Social worries. Friendship problems are a frequent hidden cause of reluctance to go to school.
  • Lack of sleep. Tired children have less patience, less focus and more worries.

3. A calmer daily routine

  1. Decompress after school. A snack, some movement or outdoor play, and no questions about grades for the first half hour.
  2. Homework at a fixed time and place, with the materials ready and screens out of sight.
  3. Short work blocks: about 10–15 minutes for younger children, 20–25 for older ones, each followed by a short break.
  4. Start with something achievable, then tackle the hardest task while energy is still good.
  5. Protect sleep: regular bedtimes and no screens in the hour before bed. Primary-school children generally need about 9 to 12 hours a night.
Try this tonightAsk your child to rate their day from 1 to 5 with their fingers, then ask "What would make tomorrow one point better?" It opens a conversation about worries without making it heavy.

4. Supporting restless or easily distracted children

Some children have more energy, more impulsivity or a shorter attention span than their classmates. Whatever the reason, these strategies help:

  • Movement first: ten minutes of running, jumping or cycling before homework often makes the next twenty minutes much calmer.
  • Make tasks visible: a checklist of three to five small steps, ticked off one by one, is easier to follow than "do your homework".
  • Use a timer: "Work until the timer rings, then a two-minute break." A clear end makes starting easier.
  • Allow quiet movement: sitting on a cushion, squeezing a soft ball or standing at a counter can help some children concentrate.
  • Give one instruction at a time, with eye contact, and ask your child to repeat it.
  • Catch good moments: specific praise ("You worked for ten minutes without stopping!") motivates far more than criticism.
Teacher's noteIn class, active children often do their best work when they have a job: handing out sheets, cleaning the board, carrying books. Giving their energy a useful purpose works better than asking them to suppress it.

5. About ADHD: when to ask for an assessment

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a recognised condition that affects attention, activity levels and impulse control. Being lively, chatty or easily bored does not mean a child has ADHD; many children go through restless phases. It may be worth asking for an assessment if:

  • difficulties with attention, restlessness or impulsivity have lasted for at least several months;
  • they appear in several places, for example both at home and at school;
  • they clearly affect learning, friendships or family life despite good routines.
ImportantOnly a qualified health professional can assess ADHD or other learning difficulties. If you are concerned, talk to your child's doctor and their teacher, and bring concrete examples. This article offers general educational guidance and does not replace medical or psychological advice.

6. Working with the school

  • Share what you see at home, and ask what the teacher sees in class. The two pictures together are much more useful.
  • Agree on one or two small adjustments: sitting closer to the teacher, shorter written tasks, extra time, or permission to move.
  • Use the same language at home and at school for routines and rewards, so the child gets consistent messages.
  • Meet again after a few weeks to see what helped.

Frequently asked questions

What are the signs of school stress in children?

Stomach aches or headaches on school days, trouble sleeping, irritability, avoiding homework, reluctance to go to school and negative statements such as "I’m bad at everything".

How can I help my restless child do homework?

Let them move before starting, break homework into a visible checklist, use a timer for short work blocks with breaks, give one instruction at a time and praise specific efforts.

When should I ask for an ADHD assessment?

If difficulties with attention, restlessness or impulsivity have lasted for several months, appear both at home and at school, and clearly affect learning or relationships. Only a qualified professional can assess ADHD.
