12 Fun Math Games That Actually Help Kids Learn

KL Kids Learning Hub · Updated October 2026 · 12 min read

The best maths practice does not look like practice. With a pack of cards, two dice, a few coins and ten minutes, you can build counting, number bonds, times tables and fractions while your child simply thinks they are playing. Here are twelve games, ordered by age, with the exact skill each one trains.

What makes a maths game worth playing

Not every game with numbers in it teaches much. A good maths game has three features: your child makes many small calculations in a short time, they have to make decisions rather than just follow luck, and the rules are simple enough to play again tomorrow without explanation. Repetition is the hidden ingredient. A game played ten times over two weeks teaches far more than a new game every day.

Games for ages 4–6

1. Dice dot race

You need: two dice, a simple track drawn on paper, two counters. Players roll and move their counter by the total. The rule that makes it educational: say the number of dots on each die without counting them one by one. Recognising small quantities at a glance, called subitising, is one of the earliest foundations of number sense.

2. Hopscotch number line

You need: chalk or masking tape. Draw a line of squares numbered 0 to 20. Call out "Start on 5, jump forward 3!" and your child hops and says where they landed. Later, add backward jumps. Children feel addition and subtraction as movement, which makes the number line meaningful when they meet it at school.

3. Make ten Go Fish

You need: a card deck with picture cards removed (ace = 1). Play like Go Fish, but a pair is two cards that add up to 10: 3 and 7, 4 and 6, ace and 9, and two 5s. "Do you have a 6?" practises exactly the number bonds that later make mental addition fast.

4. Shop and pay

You need: a few toys with price labels from 1 to 10 and real or play coins. Your child buys items and pays the exact amount, then you swap roles. Counting money combines counting, adding and the idea that one coin can be worth several units.

Games for ages 6–9

5. Card battle (three versions)

You need: a deck of cards without picture cards. Each player turns over a card; the bigger number wins both. Version two: turn over two cards each and add them. Version three, for ages 8 and up: multiply them. The same simple game grows with your child for years.

6. Guess my number

Think of a number between 1 and 100. Your child asks questions that can only be answered "yes" or "no": "Is it more than 50?", "Is it even?", "Does it have 7 tens?" Count the questions and try to beat the record. This game builds place value and logical thinking, and children soon discover that halving the range is the best strategy.

7. Race to 100

You need: one die, paper. On each turn, roll and decide whether the number is worth tens or ones: a 4 can be 40 or 4. Add it to your running total. The first to reach exactly 100, or the closest without going over after eight rolls, wins. Every turn is a decision about place value.

8. Estimation jar

Fill a jar with pasta, buttons or sweets. Everyone writes an estimate, then you count together, grouping in tens. The closest estimate wins. Over a few weeks, estimates become remarkably accurate, and counting in groups of ten becomes a habit.

Games for ages 9–12

9. Times-table bingo

Each player draws a 3×3 grid and fills it with answers from the tables they are learning (12, 24, 36, 42, 56…). The caller reads out questions such as "6 × 7". If you have 42, cross it out. Three in a row wins. Children choose numbers strategically, which makes them think about which products appear most often.

10. Make 24

You need: a deck of cards. Turn over four cards and try to reach exactly 24 using each number once with +, −, × and ÷. With 2, 3, 4 and 6, one solution is 6 × 4 × (3 − 2) = 24. Encourage children to explain their solution aloud. There are often several, and finding them builds flexible thinking about operations and brackets.

11. Paper-plate fractions

Cut paper plates into halves, quarters, thirds and eighths, and colour each set differently. Ask: "How many quarters make a half? Which is bigger, two thirds or three quarters?" Then play "fraction snap": turn over cards showing fractions and slap when two are equal, such as 2/4 and 1/2.

12. Double the recipe

Choose a simple recipe for four people and ask your child to adapt it for eight, then for two, then for six. Multiplying and dividing quantities such as 150 g or ¾ cup gives real meaning to proportional reasoning, and the reward is something to eat.

A note on game 10Not every set of four cards has a solution, and that is fine. Agreeing that "this one is impossible" after a real search is also mathematical reasoning. For a first try, deal 3, 8, 1 and 1: 3 × 8 × 1 × 1 = 24.

How to get the most out of game time

  • Play the same game several days in a row. The second and third time are when the learning really happens, because the rules no longer use up attention.
  • Talk about strategy. After a round, ask "Why did you choose that?" Explaining a decision deepens understanding.
  • Let your child win sometimes, but not always. Losing occasionally and trying again builds resilience, which matters as much in maths as any fact.
  • Keep it short. Ten to fifteen minutes is ideal. Stop while it is still fun.
  • Adjust the numbers, not the game. If it is too easy, use bigger numbers; if it is too hard, remove some cards. Familiar rules with the right challenge are the perfect combination.

Frequently asked questions

What are good maths games for 5-year-olds?

Simple dice and card games work best at this age: racing a counter with two dice, hopscotch on a number line, playing shop with coins, or a Go Fish variant where pairs add up to 10.

Do maths games really help children learn?

Yes, when the game involves many small calculations, real decisions and is played repeatedly. Playing the same game several times over a week or two builds fluency more than constantly changing games.

How long should children play maths games each day?

Ten to fifteen minutes a day is ideal. Short, regular sessions keep motivation high and give memory time to consolidate between sessions.
