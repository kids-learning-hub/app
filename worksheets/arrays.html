An array — dots arranged in equal rows — is the clearest picture of multiplication there is. Three rows of five is 3 × 5, and turning the page shows five rows of three: the same 15 dots. Children who learn the times tables from arrays understand why 3 × 5 equals 5 × 3, and later why area is length times width.

🔵 Best for ages 6–9

How to get the most from these sheets

  • Build arrays first with coins, buttons or an egg box. Ask "how many rows? how many in each row?" before "how many altogether?".
  • Agree on a convention and keep it: rows first, then the number in each row. Consistency matters more than which order you choose.
  • After writing the multiplication, turn the sheet sideways and ask for the other one. It is the fastest way to halve the facts to learn.
  • Encourage counting in rows ("5, 10, 15") rather than dot by dot. That is skip counting, and it is the step towards remembering the fact.

Frequently asked questions

What is a multiplication array?

A rectangle of objects or dots in equal rows. The number of rows times the number in each row gives the total, which makes multiplication something a child can see and count.

Why teach multiplication with arrays?

Because they show what multiplication means, prove that the order of the factors does not matter, and prepare for area and for written multiplication methods.

When should children use arrays?

From about 6 or 7, when multiplication is first introduced, and alongside the times tables until about 9. They remain useful whenever a fact is forgotten.
