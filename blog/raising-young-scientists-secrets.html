Raising Young Scientists: How to Nurture Curiosity and Critical Thinking at Home

KL Kids Learning Hub · Updated October 2026 · 10 min read

Children are born scientists. They drop things to see what happens, ask "why?" a hundred times a day and test the limits of everything around them. Science at school will give them knowledge, but the habits of a scientist — asking good questions, observing carefully, testing fairly and changing their mind when the evidence says so — grow best at home, in everyday conversations.

1. Five habits of a scientific mind

HabitWhat it sounds like
Curiosity"I wonder why the moon changes shape."
Careful observation"The ants are all walking in a line towards the bread."
Prediction"I think the ice will melt faster in the sun."
Fair testing"Let's change only one thing and keep everything else the same."
Following evidence"I thought heavy things sink, but this big log floats. So it's not just weight."

The last habit is the most precious: being willing to say "I was wrong, the evidence shows something else." Children who see adults do this learn that changing your mind is a strength, not a weakness.

2. How to answer "why?" questions

You do not need to know every answer. Some of the best science conversations start with "I don't know — how could we find out?"

  • Turn the question back: "What do you think? Why might that be?" Listen to your child's theory before giving yours.
  • Find out together: a book, a documentary, a quick test in the kitchen or a question for a teacher.
  • Keep a question wall: stick big questions on the fridge ("Why is the sky blue?") and investigate one each week.
  • Give simple, true answers for young children, and add detail as they grow. "Plants make their food using sunlight" is a fine start.
Teacher's noteChildren's "wrong" ideas about science are rarely random. "Heavy things fall faster" or "the sun goes around the Earth" are logical conclusions from what they see. Asking "What makes you think that?" before correcting helps them actually change their idea, instead of just memorising a new sentence.

3. Everyday science, no lab required

  • Nature walks with a mission: find five kinds of leaves, count the birds, look under stones for insects (and put them back gently).
  • A nature or weather journal: draw the moon every evening for a month, record temperature and clouds, watch a plant grow.
  • Kitchen science conversations: why does bread rise, why does salt melt ice, why does oil float on water?
  • Take things apart: an old clock or radio (unplugged, with adult supervision) shows that machines are made of understandable parts.
  • Sky watching: spot the moon's phases, find a bright planet, notice where the sun sets across the year.
  • Visits: science museums, botanical gardens, farms and planetariums turn questions into real experiences.
Try this tonightStart a "moon diary": every evening for two weeks, your child draws the shape of the moon on a calendar. By the end, they will have discovered the phases of the moon themselves — real observation, no explanation needed.

4. Teaching fair tests and evidence

A fair test changes only one thing at a time. It is the heart of every experiment, and children can understand it from about 7:

  1. Ask a question: "Which paper towel absorbs the most water?"
  2. Choose what to change: only the brand of paper towel.
  3. Keep everything else the same: same size of sheet, same amount of water, same time.
  4. Measure: how much water is left in the cup?
  5. Conclude: what does the evidence show? Would we get the same result if we tried again?

Older children can also learn to question claims they hear: "How do they know? Who did the test? Was it fair?" This is the beginning of critical thinking, useful far beyond science.

Safety firstSupervise any activity involving heat, sharp tools, electricity or chemicals, and teach the rule "we never taste anything in an experiment". Taking safety seriously is part of thinking like a scientist.

Frequently asked questions

How can I encourage my child’s interest in science?

Welcome their questions, investigate answers together instead of always explaining, go on nature walks with a mission, keep a moon or weather journal, and try simple kitchen experiments.

What should I say when I don’t know the answer to my child’s question?

"I don’t know — how could we find out?" Then look it up together, test it, or ask a teacher. This teaches the most important scientific habit: finding out rather than guessing.

What is a fair test for kids?

An experiment where only one thing is changed while everything else stays the same, so you can tell what caused the result. Children can understand it from about age 7.
