Developing Logical Thinking in Children: Activities, Puzzles and Games

KL Kids Learning Hub · Updated October 2026 · 10 min read

Logical thinking is the ability to reason step by step: to sort and compare, to see that "if this, then that", to draw a conclusion from clues and to explain how you know. It is the backbone of mathematics, but also of reading comprehension, science and good decisions. Children are natural reasoners; our job is to give them interesting problems and to keep asking "how do you know?"

1. The building blocks of logical thinking

Comparing and orderingBigger, smaller, first, lastPutting five sticks in order of length

3. Everyday activities

  • Odd one out: "apple, banana, carrot, pear — which doesn't belong?" Accept any answer that is well justified: carrot (not a fruit) or banana (not round) can both be right.
  • Sorting the laundry or the shopping: by colour, by owner, by type, then ask your child to invent a new sorting rule.
  • Ordering: line up family members by height, or toy cars by size, then ask "Who is between…?"
  • Cause and effect at the table: "What would happen if we left the ice cream out of the freezer?"
  • Spot the nonsense: say a silly sentence ("I put my shoes on before my socks") and let your child explain what's wrong.
Teacher's noteIn maths, the children who make the most progress are not always the fastest calculators. They are the ones who check whether an answer makes sense: "37 + 45 can't be 712, that's far too big." Ask for an estimate before every calculation and this habit grows quickly.

4. A logic puzzle to solve together

Here is a simple deduction puzzle for ages 7 and up. Draw a small grid or use three cups to keep track.

Lina, Omar and Sara each have one pet: a cat, a dog or a fish.

  • Omar's pet cannot swim.
  • Sara is allergic to cats.
  • Lina's pet lives in water.

Who has which pet? Solution: Lina's pet lives in water, so Lina has the fish. Sara cannot have the cat, so Sara has the dog. That leaves the cat for Omar, and a cat cannot swim, which matches the first clue.

Try this tonightSolve the puzzle above together, then ask your child to invent one for you with three people and three objects. Making a puzzle that has exactly one answer is harder than solving one, and teaches a lot.

5. Games that build reasoning

  • Guess Who-style games: asking questions that eliminate half the options at once.
  • Code-breaking games like Mastermind: test a guess, read the feedback, adjust.
  • Connect Four and noughts and crosses: thinking one move ahead.
  • Sudoku: pure deduction, from easy 4×4 grids to classic 9×9.
  • Tangrams and pattern blocks: spatial reasoning and trial and improvement.
  • Chess (from about 6 or 7): planning, consequences and patience.

6. The most powerful question

"How do you know?" turns any activity into a reasoning lesson. Ask it after right answers as well as wrong ones, so it never feels like a trap. Help your child use reasoning words: because, so, if… then, it can't be… because, that means. Over time, they start asking themselves the question, which is exactly what logical thinkers do.

Let them struggle a littleA puzzle that is solved immediately teaches little. If your child is stuck, give a small hint ("What do you know for sure?") rather than the answer. The effort of reasoning is where the learning happens.

Frequently asked questions

How can I develop my child’s logical thinking?

Practise sorting and comparing, play odd-one-out and deduction games, solve simple logic puzzles together, ask for estimates before calculations, and regularly ask "How do you know?"

What games help with logical thinking?

Guess Who-style games, code-breaking games like Mastermind, Connect Four, sudoku, tangrams and, from about 6 or 7, chess all build reasoning and planning.

At what age can children solve logic puzzles?

Picture-based odd-one-out and sorting puzzles work from about 4. Simple deduction puzzles with three clues suit ages 7 and up, and sudoku-style puzzles from about 7 or 8.
