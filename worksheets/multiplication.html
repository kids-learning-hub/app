Times tables are the last piece of arithmetic that has to become automatic. A child who still counts up to work out 7 × 8 will struggle with division, fractions and long multiplication later — not because those are harder, but because the working memory is already full. These sheets exist to move recall from counting to knowing.

✖️ Best for ages 7–11

How to get the most from these sheets

  • Start with the 2, 5 and 10 tables — their patterns are visible, so early wins come fast.
  • Then 3 and 4, and leave 6, 7, 8 and 9 for last: by then half of them are already known in reverse.
  • Ten minutes a day beats an hour on Sunday. Recall is built by frequency, not duration.
  • Print a fresh sheet each time rather than repeating one — memorising a page is not the same as knowing the facts.

Frequently asked questions

In what order should a child learn the times tables?

Start with 2, 5 and 10 because their patterns are obvious, then 3 and 4, and finish with 6, 7, 8 and 9. By the time you reach the hard ones, most have already appeared in reverse — a child who knows 4 × 7 already knows 7 × 4.

At what age should times tables be mastered?

Most children learn them between 7 and 9 and are fluent by about 10. Starting earlier rarely helps; short, regular practice matters far more than an early start.

How long does it take to memorise them?

With about ten minutes of daily practice, most children are secure within two to three months. Progress is uneven — the 6, 7 and 8 tables usually take as long as all the others combined.
