Emotional Intelligence in Children: Helping Kids Name, Understand and Manage Their Feelings

KL Kids Learning Hub · Updated October 2026 · 11 min read

A child who can say "I'm frustrated because my tower fell" is in a very different place from a child who throws the blocks across the room. The difference is not character; it is a set of skills that can be taught: noticing feelings, naming them, calming down, and understanding how others feel. These skills support friendships, learning and wellbeing, and parents have more influence over them than anyone.

1. The five skills of emotional intelligence

SkillWhat it looks like in a child
Self-awarenessNoticing and naming their own feelings: "I'm nervous about the test."
Self-regulationCalming down after being upset, waiting for a turn, stopping before hitting.
MotivationKeeping going when something is hard, and coping with disappointment.
EmpathyRecognising how others feel: "Sami looks sad, maybe he wants to play."
Social skillsSharing, apologising, solving disagreements with words, making friends.

None of these skills is fixed. Children build them through thousands of small everyday moments, and especially through how the adults around them react to feelings.

2. What to expect at each age

AgeTypical stageHow to help
3–5Big feelings, short fuses; can name happy, sad, angry, scared.Name feelings for them, stay calm and close, use simple calming routines.
6–8More words for feelings; starting to understand that others feel differently.Expand the vocabulary (frustrated, disappointed, jealous), talk about characters' feelings in stories.
9–12Can reflect on feelings and plan how to handle them; friendships matter a lot.Problem-solve together, discuss tricky social situations, respect their need for some privacy.

Tantrums at four and slammed doors at eleven are both normal. What changes with age is how quickly a child recovers and how well they can explain what happened afterwards.

3. Name it to tame it: building a feelings vocabulary

Simply putting a feeling into words helps a child calm down. A child who only knows "angry" uses it for everything; a child who can say "disappointed", "embarrassed" or "left out" understands themselves better and is easier to help.

  • Narrate feelings during the day: "You look proud of that drawing." "I think you're disappointed that the park is closed."
  • Use stories: pause when reading and ask "How do you think she feels? How can you tell?"
  • Make a feelings chart with faces for 8 to 10 emotions and let your child point to how they feel.
  • Share your own feelings calmly: "I'm feeling stressed because we're late. I'm going to take a deep breath."
Try this tonightAt dinner, play "Rose and thorn": everyone shares one good moment of the day (the rose) and one difficult moment (the thorn), and how it made them feel. It takes five minutes and builds the habit of talking about emotions.

4. Calming strategies children can use on their own

Teach these when your child is calm, practise them as a game, and then suggest them in difficult moments:

  • Balloon breathing: breathe in slowly through the nose to fill a "balloon" in the tummy, then let it out slowly through the mouth. Three to five times.
  • Count down from ten, or count five things they can see around them.
  • A calm corner: a cushion, a soft toy and a few books where a child can choose to go. It is a place to recover, not a punishment.
  • Move the feeling out: squeeze a cushion, jump ten times, or draw the feeling.
  • Words for the feeling: "I'm so angry!" said out loud is much better than a fist.

5. A script for big emotions

When your child is overwhelmed, a predictable sequence helps both of you:

  1. Stay calm and close. Your calm voice is the most powerful tool. A child cannot calm down with an angry adult.
  2. Name the feeling: "You're really angry that your brother took your car."
  3. Accept the feeling, limit the behaviour: "It's okay to be angry. It's not okay to hit."
  4. Help them calm down: breathing together, a hug if they want one, or quiet time nearby.
  5. Solve the problem later: once calm, talk about what happened and what they could do next time.
Teacher's noteIn class, the children who handle conflicts best are rarely the ones who never get angry. They are the ones who have learned what to do next. Practising the "next step" in calm moments is what makes it available in hard ones.

6. Building empathy and friendship skills

  • Ask perspective questions: "How do you think your friend felt when that happened?"
  • Role-play tricky situations with toys: joining a game, saying no kindly, apologising.
  • Notice kindness: "You shared your snack, and Lina smiled. That was kind."
  • Teach a real apology: say what you did, how it made the other person feel, and how you will fix it.

7. Mistakes to avoid

  • Dismissing feelings: "Stop crying, it's nothing" teaches children to hide feelings, not to manage them.
  • Punishing the feeling instead of the behaviour: anger is allowed; hurting others is not.
  • Lecturing in the middle of a meltdown: the thinking brain is offline. Talk later.
  • Fixing every problem for them: let older children try solutions first, and support them if they need it.
When to ask for helpIf your child's emotions often lead to aggression, if they seem persistently sad or anxious, or if their feelings stop them from going to school or making friends, talk to their teacher and your doctor. Frequently asked questions

What is emotional intelligence in children?

It is the set of skills that let a child notice and name feelings, calm down, understand how others feel and get along with people. These skills can be taught and improve with practice.

How can I help my child manage big emotions?

Stay calm and close, name the feeling, accept the feeling while limiting harmful behaviour, help your child calm down with breathing or a quiet space, and talk about solutions once they are calm.

At what age can children name their feelings?

Most children can name basic feelings such as happy, sad, angry and scared by about 3 or 4. Richer words like frustrated, disappointed or embarrassed usually come between 6 and 8 with adult help.
