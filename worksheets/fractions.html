A fraction is a number, not a pair of numbers. Children only see that once they have looked at many shapes cut into equal parts and asked the same two questions every time: how many equal parts is the whole cut into, and how many of them are shaded? These sheets ask exactly that, with pies and bars side by side, so that 2/4 and 1/2 can be seen to cover the same amount.

🍕 Best for ages 6–10

How to get the most from these sheets

  • Start with halves and quarters only. They are the fractions children meet in daily life — half an apple, a quarter of an hour.
  • Say the fraction aloud as "three out of four equal parts" before writing 3/4. The words carry the meaning the symbols cannot yet.
  • Point out that the parts must be equal. Folding paper into halves and quarters makes this concrete in two minutes.
  • Use the answer key to discuss mistakes: a child who writes 1/3 for one part out of four is counting the unshaded parts, a very common confusion.

Frequently asked questions

At what age do children learn fractions?

Halves and quarters appear around 6 or 7, thirds and sixths around 8, and comparing or adding fractions from about 9 or 10. Shapes and folding come long before written rules.

Why use both pies and bars?

Because each hides something the other shows. Pies make "part of a whole" obvious; bars make it easier to compare sizes and later to place fractions on a number line.

My child writes the unshaded parts. What should I do?

Ask two separate questions every time: how many equal parts in total, then how many are coloured. Write the total underneath first; the shaded count goes on top.
